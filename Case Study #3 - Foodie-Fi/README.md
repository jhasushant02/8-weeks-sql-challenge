# Case Study #3: Foodie-Fi

**Tool:** DuckDB | **Skills:** event-log analysis, window functions (LEAD, LAG, ROW_NUMBER), conditional aggregation, string_agg, recursive CTEs, date arithmetic

## The question

Danny launched Foodie-Fi in 2020, a streaming service for cooking shows with monthly and annual plans. Before making bigger bets on features, pricing and investment, he wants the decisions backed by data: how customers move through trials, upgrades, downgrades and churn, and what each plan actually brings in.

## The data

Two tables: `plans` and `subscriptions`. See [`data/`](data/).

## Approach

- **Event log, not a snapshot.** One row in `subscriptions` is one plan change. A customer's current plan is their latest row, so I order by `start_date` before aggregating.
- **Journeys as strings.** `string_agg(... ORDER BY start_date)` collapses each customer's rows into one path such as `0 → 1 → 4`. Matching on that string answers "churned straight after trial" without self-joins.
- **Next-plan questions use `LEAD`.** Post-trial plan mix and downgrades compare each row with the customer's next row.
- **Point-in-time questions use `ROW_NUMBER`.** For the plan mix at 2020-12-31, I keep only rows on or before the date and take each customer's latest.
- **Time to upgrade with `FILTER`.** `MIN(start_date) FILTER (WHERE plan_id = ...)` puts the trial date and annual date on one row per customer, then `date_diff` and `FLOOR(days / 30)` build the buckets.
- **Payments table with a recursive CTE.** Each paid plan starts a payment calendar that steps forward month by month and stops at the next plan change, churn, or 2020-12-31. Basic-to-pro upgrades get a credit, and pro-monthly-to-annual switches are deferred to the next billing anniversary.

Full queries: [`analysis/solutions.sql`](analysis/solutions.sql). Results: [`output/results.md`](output/results.md).

## What I found

- **1,000 customers; 307 churned (30.7%).** 92 of them (9%) left straight after the free trial without ever paying.
- **Most trials convert to basic:** 54.6% go to basic monthly, 32.5% to pro monthly, 3.7% to pro annual, and 9.2% churn.
- **At 2020-12-31 pro monthly is the biggest group (326),** ahead of churned (236), basic monthly (224) and pro annual (195). 19 customers were still in trial.
- **195 customers upgraded to annual in 2020, taking about 105 days on average** from trial start. The 30-day buckets show when in the first year those upgrades happen.
- **No customer went from pro monthly down to basic monthly in 2020.** Downgrades are rare here; leaving is the more common way out.
- **Trial sign-ups are steady month to month** (68 to 94), so growth comes from retention and plan mix rather than a seasonal spike.
- **Journeys group into four types:** settle on a plan, go straight to premium, upgrade over time, or convert and later churn.

## Design note

`subscriptions` only records when a plan starts, so the end of a plan has to be inferred from the next row. A real system would also store an `end_date` and the billing period, plus a separate `payments` table fed by the billing system rather than reconstructed from plan dates.

## Takeaway

Define what one row represents before counting anything. Here a row is a plan change, so every question needs the customer's timeline in order first: churn, conversion and "current plan" only make sense once the rows are sequenced.

## Limitations

- Data covers all of 2020 but only part of 2021, so "after 2020" results are a partial year.
- The `payments` table is rebuilt from plan dates and Danny's four billing rules, not from real transactions.
- Monthly payment dates are generated by adding one month to the previous payment date. For customers who start on the 29th to 31st, DuckDB's end-of-month handling can shift later payments by a day or two (for example 01-30 becomes 02-29, then 03-29). Anchoring each payment to the original start date plus N months would fix this.
- The ratio questions have no "why". The table shows what changed and when, never the reason.
